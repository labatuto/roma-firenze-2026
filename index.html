Roma, Con Calma

The acclimation day. Pasta, fountains, and a 2,600-year-old prison if you’re feeling ambitious.

Morning — the market on your doorstep Idea
Campo de’ Fiori market

Runs until ~2 PM, literally outside the door of no. 27 — fruit, flowers, and a vendor who will absolutely overcharge you for a lemon the size of Rafa’s head. Worth it.

The brooding hooded statue in the middle is Giordano Bruno, burned on this spot in 1600 for insisting, among other heresies, that the universe was infinite. The Church apologized in 2000. He still looks unconvinced.

9:00 AM slot — flexible all day Booked
Mamertine Prison (Carcer Tullianum)

Nancy has tickets for 9 AM, but they’ll honor them any time before 5 PM — so go when the naps allow. A 20–30 minute visit to Rome’s state dungeon: Jugurtha starved here, Vercingetorix was strangled here after Caesar’s triumph, and tradition holds that Peter and Paul were chained here before their martyrdoms. Heavy material, short visit, air-conditioned by virtue of being a hole in the ground.

Cats, the Pantheon, and the first gelato

The Colosseum & the Forum

Bread and circuses in the morning; church in the evening.

10:00 AM — meet the guide Booked
Colosseum & Roman Forum tour — about 3 hours

Nancy has the tickets and the meeting point. Note the time: 10:00 AM. This tour was rescheduled twice — an earlier version of the family plan said 12:15 PM, but the tour operator’s confirmation of June 9 says 10:00 AM. Believe the most recent email; ask Nancy on WhatsApp if anyone’s unsure.

Fifty thousand seats, free admission, and the emperor watching from the box — the Colosseum was the original public entertainment policy. The Forum next door is where the Republic actually happened: the rostra where Cicero spoke, the Senate house, the temple where Caesar’s body was burned. It is also, in June, a shadeless rock field — hats and water.

The Vatican

Seven kilometers of galleries, one ceiling.

10:00 AM – 1:00 PM Booked
Vatican tour — museums & Sistine Chapel

Meeting point near the Vatican — Nancy has the confirmation (it’s in TripIt). Roughly 3 hours.

Dress code, enforced at security with no exceptions: shoulders AND knees covered — applies to every adult and teenager. Shorts must reach the knee; bring light scarves for cover-ups. Bring passports. No photos inside the Sistine Chapel.

The family homework was The Agony and the Ecstasy. No spoilers, but Michelangelo finishes the ceiling — four years on his back, paint in his eyes, while Julius II yelled at him to hurry up. You get about 15 minutes under it before the guards shuffle you along. Use them.

1:15 PM — the correct lunch Idea
Pizzarium

Gabriele Bonci’s legendary pizza al taglio, Via della Meloria 43, five minutes from the museum exit. Sold by weight, eaten standing or on benches, roughly €7–9 a person. The single best post-Vatican move in Rome. The line moves fast.

How to spend a last morning in Rome

Trevi Fountain before the crowds — new for 2026, the fountain’s lower basin costs €2 (free under 6)… unless you go before 9 AM, when it’s free and nearly empty. Coin over the left shoulder: it means you’ll return to Rome.

Villa Borghese gardens — Rome’s Central Park. Rowboats on the little lake (€3/person per 20 min, there’s a temple and there are turtles), pedal carts, shade. The toddler-decompression valve before strapping him into a train seat.

The Ghetto, one last time — lunch at Nonna Betta or Piperno; carciofi alla giudia don’t exist in Florence.

Morning — beat the heat Ideas
Baptistery & the Museum (both on your pass)

The Baptistery — the oldest building on the square, where every Florentine from Dante onward was baptized — and then the Opera del Duomo Museum, the sleeper hit of Florence: Ghiberti’s original Gates of Paradise, Donatello’s gaunt wooden Magdalene, and the Pietà Michelangelo carved for his own tomb and then attacked with a hammer. The museum is air-conditioned, which by 11 AM will feel like its greatest masterpiece.

Lunch Idea
Mercato Centrale

Ten people, ten different cravings, zero reservations: the food hall upstairs at the Mercato Centrale (10-minute walk) has a counter for everyone — fresh pasta, pizza, lampredotto for the brave, gelato. Long shared tables, high chairs, elevator, A/C. This is the default answer to “where should ten people eat?” for the rest of the trip.

6:00 PM sharp — the main event Booked
The Dome climb — 463 steps

Timed entry, no flexibility, photo ID checked at the gate (Porta della Mandorla, north side). You climb between the inner and outer shells of Brunelleschi’s dome — the largest masonry dome ever built, raised without scaffolding by a goldsmith who refused to explain his methods — and come out 90 meters above Florence in the early evening light.

Who climbs: ideally every adult — the family is working on a babysitter for the evening so nobody has to sit this one out. The stairs are narrow, spiraling, and one-way; not for Rafa or Lucia.

Worth knowing while you stand there
The Pazzi conspiracy happened under this dome

April 26, 1478, during High Mass: assassins backed by a rival banking family (and, awkwardly, the Pope) attacked the Medici brothers at the moment of the elevation of the Host. Giuliano died on the cathedral floor with nineteen stab wounds; Lorenzo vaulted the choir rail and barricaded himself in the sacristy, and lived to hunt down every conspirator. They hanged from the windows of the Palazzo Vecchio you’ll walk past tonight. Machiavelli was nine years old and took the lesson.

Evening Ideas
Dinner near the dome

Trattoria Zà Zà (on Nicole & Santi’s saved map — big menu, big tables, tourist-adjacent but reliably good) or Osteria Belle Donne (Emily’s pick, charming room). Both take reservations — book this morning for tonight.

The Open Day

The museum question Check
The Uffizi — still unticketed

As of when this guide was compiled, nobody holds Uffizi tickets — it’s on the family to-do list. June slots sell out, but returns appear daily. Two honest options:

Book it: uffizi.it, official site, ~€29/adult, under-18 free (still needs a slot). The 8:15 AM slot is coolest and emptiest; the new €16 “after 4 PM” ticket is the budget hack. With kids, do the 90-minute version: Giotto → Botticelli’s Birth of Venus and Primavera → Leonardo’s Annunciation → the terrace café over the Palazzo Vecchio, done.

Or skip it honestly: with a 3-year-old and an infant in mid-June heat, there is no shame in choosing the Boboli Gardens over four floors of Madonnas. The paintings have waited 500 years; they’ll wait for the next trip.

The other gallery Idea
The Accademia — David

One hallway, one statue, 45 minutes, and even Rafa will get it — it’s a 17-foot giant. (That’s rather the point: David was the underdog. Florence, surrounded by larger powers, adopted him as the civic mascot.) Same booking reality as the Uffizi: official site, under-18 free, book a morning slot or go at 7 PM when the day-trippers leave.

The escape-the-city options Ideas
Tuscany, scaled to the crew

Fiesole — the realistic one. City bus #7 from Piazza San Marco, 25 minutes, and you’re in a hill town with a Roman theater, Etruscan walls, and a view over all of Florence. Half a day, stroller-tolerant, gelato available. This is the day trip that doesn’t end in tears.

Siena — the ambitious one. ~1.5 hours by bus (the 131R from the train station is direct), the Campo, the striped Duomo, medieval streets. A full day; the littles will spend a lot of it being carried.

San Gimignano — the postcard one. Medieval Manhattan, fourteen stone towers. Requires bus connections via Poggibonsi; honestly hard with ten people and a stroller.

A cooking class — Nancy floated this in January. Several outfits do family pasta-making (~€60–90/person, 3 hours, you eat what you make). Rafa rolling pici with flour to his elbows is the photo of the trip; book a morning class via GetYourGuide/Airbnb Experiences if the family votes yes.

The do-nothing option Idea
Boboli Gardens & the Oltrarno

Cross the Ponte Vecchio before 9 AM (empty, golden), then the Boboli Gardens — the Medici’s backyard, 45 hectares of cypress avenues, grottos, and fountains where kids can actually run. Lunch in the Oltrarno (La Casalinga or Osteria Santo Spirito), gelato at Gelateria della Passera, and home for naps. This is the option your correspondent would pick.

The Last Full Day

Markets in the morning, the whole city at sunset, suitcases at night.

Morning Ideas
Mercato Centrale, ground floor — provisioning run

The real market (closes 2 PM, closed Sunday): aged pecorino, dried porcini, good olive oil, truffle things. US customs note: hard cheese (pecorino, parmesan) is allowed home; cured meats (salami, prosciutto) are not — they will be confiscated and you will be sad. Buy the cheese, photograph the salami, let it go.

Also this morning: Santa Croce (€9.50, under-12 free) — the tombs of Michelangelo, Galileo, and Machiavelli under one roof, with the calcio storico arena built out front (the final is June 24; you’ll see the sand and bleachers and be glad you’re not in it).

Golden hour — ~7:45 PM Idea
Piazzale Michelangelo

The sunset view of Florence — the whole city, the dome, the hills, in one frame. Taxi up (~€12, you cannot make ten people climb in this heat), claim balustrade space by 8 PM (sunset ~8:50), then walk down through the rose garden into San Niccolò for a last dinner. There is a bronze David up there; he has the best office in Italy.

Artisans & Heirlooms

Shop

From Santi & Nicole’s “Italy Trip 2026” list — things made by hand, by people who’ve been making them for generations. Nearly everything below is a short walk from the Florence apartment. Buy the thing you’ll still own in thirty years.

Leather & Gloves — Florence

  • Scuola del Cuoio — the leather school in the back of Santa Croce; watch the artisans work, then buy from them directly.
  • Madova — glove-makers since 1919, by the Ponte Vecchio. The “century-old affordable glove shop” Nicole flagged in February. Cashmere-lined, fitted to your hand at the counter.
  • Il Bisonte — Florentine leather goods since 1970; the unlined totes age beautifully.
  • Benheart — handmade jackets and belts, Via Calzaiuoli.
  • The Bridge Boutique — recommended by Jon Arbuach.
  • Frau Leman — handmade leather.

Paper, Prints & Art Supplies — Florence

  • Il Papiro — Florentine marbled paper; the notebooks make the best gifts under €20 in the city.
  • Zecchi Colori Belle Arti — the oldest art-supply store in Italy; pigments the Renaissance painters used. Buy Rafa his Italy sketchbook refill here.
  • Alberto Cozzi — bookbinding and paper restoration, fourth generation.
  • Giovanni Baccani — The Blue Shop — prints and framing since 1903.
  • Florence Factory — contemporary craft; good ceramics.

Scent & Apothecary — Florence

  • Officina Profumo-Farmaceutica di Santa Maria Novella — founded by Dominican friars in 1612; the oldest pharmacy in the world. The rose water and the pot-pourri. Worth visiting just for the frescoed halls.
  • Profumeria Invicta — perfume and hair accessories.

Books, Design & Home — Florence

  • Todo Modo — bookstore with a café and a theater hidden in back.
  • Gucci Giardino — the surprisingly good bookstore inside the Gucci garden.
  • Biblioteca Marucelliana — not a shop; a 1752 reading room you can walk into for free. For Eleni’s study sessions.
  • Seletti Store — playful design objects.
  • Artigiani Toscana — Tuscan home goods.
  • Merceria Firenze Delcor — notions and classic pajamas.
  • The Way of Tea 2 — tea shop, for the post-trip ritual.

Rome — Two Stops Only

  • Gammarelli — Via di Santa Chiara 34, behind the Pantheon. Tailors to the popes since 1798. You cannot buy the white cassock, but you can buy the cardinal-red socks, and you should. (Nicole’s find — “if we pass by we should stop in!”)
  • Stadio di Domiziano — not a shop; the excavated stadium under Piazza Navona that Nancy flagged (“supposed to be great”). €8, half an hour, and you understand why the piazza is shaped like that.

Rules of Engagement

  • Real artisan shops wrap things for travel — ask, they’re proud of it.
  • The leather stalls around San Lorenzo market are not the same as the shops above. Fine for a belt; not for the thing you keep.
  • Tax refund (IVA): purchases over €70 at one shop can claim ~12% back at the airport — keep receipts, ask for the form.
  • Carry-on math: you flew home with no checked bags. Gloves and paper fold; ceramics do not.
Logistics & Field Notes

The Survival Guide

Ten people, two of them under four, in a 95-degree June. Here is how it works anyway.

Money

  • Get cash from bank ATMs (Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit — attached to branches), never the blue Euronet machines. Decline “conversion to USD” every time — always charge in euros.
  • Carry €100–150 cash: the Rome city tax (€5/person/night, cash), Trattoria Mario (cash only), market stalls, gelato.
  • Cards work everywhere else; contactless is universal.
  • Coperto is the per-person cover charge: normal, legal, not a tip, not a scam.

Babies & Pharmacies

  • Diapers = pannolini; wipes = salviettine; formula = latte in polvere (Aptamil “tipo 1” for Lucia’s age). Supermarkets (Conad, Coop) are cheapest; pharmacies are everywhere and the pharmacists speak English.
  • 24-hour pharmacies: Farmacia della Stazione at Roma Termini; Farmacia Comunale inside Florence SMN station.
  • Italian pharmacists can diagnose and dispense for minor kid ailments — they are the urgent care.
  • Emergency number: 112 (EU-wide, English-speaking operators).

Evening Culture for the Siblings

  • Rome: Trastevere after 10 PM, Bar San Callisto for €3 beers, Il Goccetto for wine; the Lungo il Tevere river festival runs nightly (free entry, skip the food).
  • Florence: Piazza Santo Spirito is where the city actually hangs out; Volume and Pop Café for spritz.
  • Sunday option: the Appian Way is closed to cars — rent e-bikes and ride the 2,300-year-old road out past the aqueducts. Back by tour time.
  • Calcio storico (Florentine costumed rugby-brawling) finals are June 24 — you’ll miss the match but the arena in Piazza Santa Croce will be up all week.
